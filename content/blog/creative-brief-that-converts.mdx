Weak ads are usually a briefing problem, not a talent problem. Hand a brilliant designer a vague brief and you'll get beautiful work that doesn't convert. Give them a sharp one and the same designer produces a scroll-stopper.

Here's the framework we use before anyone opens a design file.

## Start with the one thing

If the viewer remembers only one thing from this ad, what should it be? Not three things. One. A brief that tries to say everything says nothing.

## Answer five questions

Every brief we write answers these, in plain language:

- **Who** is this for — specifically, not "everyone"?
- **What** do they believe right now, and what do we want them to believe instead?
- **Why** should they trust us — the proof, the number, the result?
- **What** is the single action we want them to take?
- **Where** will this run, and what does that platform reward?

> The best brief reads like a clear argument, not a wish list. If your team has to guess your intent, they'll guess wrong.

## Give proof, not adjectives

"Premium" and "innovative" mean nothing. "181M in sales in two months" means everything. Hand over the real numbers, quotes, and outcomes — they're the raw material of persuasion.

## Show, don't just tell

Include a reference or two of what "good" looks like, and be honest about what you don't want. A single example saves a dozen rounds of revisions.

## Leave room for craft

Brief the *what* and the *why*. Let the creative team own the *how*. The magic lives in the execution — micromanage it and you lose exactly what you hired them for.

---

A tight brief is the highest-leverage 30 minutes in the whole campaign. Spend it well, and everything downstream gets easier.
